Since 2022, we have carried the modal and numerical tools first developed for light over to mechanical waves: flexural vibrations of thin plates loaded with resonators, discrete phononic lattices and airborne sound.

## Quasinormal modes of thin elastic plates

Elastic wave control with large arrays of resonators calls for advanced simulation and optimization methods. We develop a quasinormal mode (QNM) framework for thin elastic plates, where the Green's function formalism readily solves multiple scattering problems. The associated nonlinear eigenvalue problem yields a dispersive QNM expansion: a reduced order model that computes forced responses efficiently and shows how each resonant mode is excited. Eigenvalue sensitivities with respect to the resonator parameters then drive a gradient-based optimization that designs quasi-bound states in the continuum and places eigenfrequencies precisely in the complex plane. Scattering simulations on graded line arrays and quasicrystals validate the approach. The same theory, applied to electromagnetic resonators, lives under [Quasi-Normal Modes](/research/qnms/).

## Topological edge states

Topological waveguides that support quantum valley Hall edge states confine waves to an interface and resist backscattering, even in the presence of defects. They are usually studied as an infinite spectral problem, but real devices are finite. We apply the quasinormal mode expansion to a finite hexagonal lattice of mass-spring resonators on a thin plate. The resulting complex, discrete spectrum fully describes the topological states, and the waveguide behaves as a dispersive Fabry-Pérot cavity whose dispersion relation follows closely that of the infinite (Floquet-Bloch) ribbon.

## Discrete phononic lattices

Networks of masses and springs are the simplest phononic crystals, and their dispersion relations can be computed and optimized at very low cost. We modeled such lattices and optimized their dispersion, including in a 2023 undergraduate summer project at Imperial College London. I wrote pylatt to compute and optimize them.

## Sound absorption with ceramic tiles

We turn ceramic-tiled walls into broadband sound absorbers by exploiting their own geometry: the 1 mm empty joints between tiles act as resonator necks, and the gap between tiles and wall acts as the resonator chamber. Spatial grading of these resonators gives broadband absorption between 100 and 1500 Hz, the range of footsteps, impacts and neighboring activity. A structure only 1 cm thick reaches an average absorption coefficient above 0.7, with near-unity absorption between 480 and 700 Hz, and numerical modeling, analytical formulation and experiments agree.
